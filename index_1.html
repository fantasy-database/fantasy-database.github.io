Reading the grid

Each cell is the opponent and where it is played — (H) home, (A) away — over the model's rating for that fixture.

Shading is continuous rather than five buckets. The model produces a real number for every fixture, so bucketing it into bands would throw away the precision that made it worth building.

The two settings

Opponents asks how bad the teams they play are, and nothing else. It is the direct replacement for FDR, and it will happily put a poor side at the top if their fixtures are kind.

Expected returns asks what this team should get out of those fixtures, using their own attack and defence too. Most of the time this is the one you want. A side can top one list and sit near the bottom of the other.

How it is built

Teams are rated on non-penalty expected goals, because a penalty belongs to whoever takes it rather than to a side's ability to create chances. Penalties are added back at league rate (×1.06) when a rating becomes a goal projection.

The constants are fitted, not chosen. Splitting last season's 760 matches into odd and even halves and correlating each team against itself measures how much of a sample is signal; k = n(1−r)/r turns that into a blend constant. Attack came out at 8, defence at 26, home advantage at 1.11×.

Every rating is opponent-adjusted: each match is measured against what an average side would have managed versus that opponent, at that venue, so a soft or brutal run of fixtures does not masquerade as form.

The three promoted sides, marked , have no Premier League record. Their prior is what promoted sides have actually managed in their first season up, averaged over the fifteen teams promoted in the last five seasons — 1.11 npxG and 1.95 npxGA per game. Their k is the one constant still set by judgement rather than fitted.

Caveats

Two matches is a very small sample. Squad turnover is not modelled at all — a side that sold its striker in August is not last season's side, and no amount of xG history knows that.
